01 About

My work sits across evaluation and rewards, human feedback and preference-based learning, and data-centric methods for training large language models. In practice, that has meant building benchmarks that expose a specific failure mode: an answer, a caption, or a judge's score that reads as correct but isn't, in Arabic visual question answering, in culturally grounded reasoning, and in LLM-as-judge evaluation. It has also meant building and running KAUST OPTIMAL Lab's Saudi-dialect preference-optimization pipeline (DPO, APO, RLOO), and the evaluation needed to tell whether any of it actually worked.

RL-based post-training and alignment is the other side of that interest: the same question, asked of a reward instead of a label. My independent RL work tests it directly, for example in a multi-agent social dilemma I built, where an inequity-averse reward meant to encourage cooperation made resource collapse worse instead of better.

I don't fully trust a result until I've audited it by hand. It's a habit that keeps paying off quietly: the case I almost didn't bother checking is usually the one that mattered.

03 Research interests

The four areas my work keeps coming back to:

RL-based post-training and alignment

Training language models with reward signals instead of static labels (DPO, APO, RLOO), and tracking what that training actually changes versus what it just appears to change.

Evaluation and rewards

Benchmarks and judges built to check whether a score can be trusted, not just reported as a number.

Human feedback and preference-based learning

What a model learns from being told which answer is better, as opposed to which one is correct, and where that distinction quietly breaks down.

Data-centric methods for LLM training

What goes into a model before training starts: corpus curation, dialectal coverage, and how early data choices resurface later in evaluation.

04 Projects

Two tracks: benchmarks I'm building as research, and a set of RL fundamentals I'm working through on my own to carry the evaluation question into reward signals.

Research benchmarks

Fatan

LLM Evaluation

Culturally Aligned vs. Culturally Competent: a benchmark testing whether multimodal LLMs genuinely understand Gulf/Islamic cultural context or just pattern-match plausible answers. Three-tier design (MCQ, open-ended, "none of the above") built specifically to catch models that guess well on multiple choice but fall apart once the safety net of a correct option is removed.

Dataset ↗

Sarab

LLM Evaluation

An Arabic cause-diagnostic visual-hallucination benchmark that tests not just whether a model hallucinates on Arabic visual questions but why: visual ambiguity, multimodal inconsistency, or incorrect context, each isolated by construction rather than inferred after the fact. 650+ triplets across four modes, built around an original Arabic cultural taxonomy spanning attire, cuisine, architecture, cultural objects, and script.

Reinforcement learning · self-directed study

social-dilemma-rl

Reinforcement Learning

Independent tabular Q-learning agents in sequential social dilemmas: iterated matrix games and a spatial tragedy-of-the-commons gridworld. Includes a real, reported negative result: inequity-averse reward shaping made resource collapse worse here, with a concrete hypothesis for why, instead of a result quietly dropped.

Repository ↗

tabular-control-rl

Reinforcement Learning

Exact dynamic programming (value iteration, policy iteration) checked against sample-based TD control (Q-learning, SARSA) on Cliff Walking and Windy Gridworld, verified to converge on the exact same optimum and confirmed independently with a plain BFS. Reproduces the classic result that Q-learning learns the risky optimal path while SARSA learns a safer one and pays less for its own exploration.

Repository ↗

function-approx-rl

Reinforcement Learning

Linear function approximation (tile coding) with semi-gradient SARSA on Mountain Car, the standard example of a control problem too large for a tabular method to represent. Learns to pump momentum from scratch, cutting steps-to-goal by ~7.6x, and includes a step-size sweep that reports a real plateau instead of the instability I went in expecting to find.

Repository ↗

deep-q-rl

Reinforcement Learning

DQN built entirely from scratch: a two-layer MLP with the backward pass derived and coded by hand in numpy, no autograd, trained with experience replay and a target network on a self-implemented CartPole. Correctness is checked with a finite-difference gradient test, not just "training looks fine," and an ablation shows the target network isn't a nice-to-have here: removing it caused outright numerical divergence, not just slower learning.

Repository ↗

gradient-td-rl

Reinforcement Learning

Baird's counterexample (Baird, 1995), reproduced to show that naive off-policy semi-gradient TD genuinely diverges: MSPBE goes from 67.6 to 4.17e+22 over 20,000 steps, while GTD2 and TDC (Sutton, Maei & Precup, 2009) provably converge to the true fixed point instead. The Mean-Squared Projected Bellman Error is computed in exact closed form via linear algebra, not estimated from samples, which also surfaced a real wrinkle: Baird's 8-feature matrix is rank-deficient, requiring a pseudoinverse rather than a direct solve.

Repository ↗

policy-gradient-rl

Reinforcement Learning

REINFORCE (Williams, 1992) and the Policy Gradient Theorem on Sutton & Barto's short-corridor task, where only a stochastic policy can win because the agent can't tell states apart. The task's exact performance is solvable in closed form, which turns "is this estimator really unbiased" into something directly checkable, and caught a real bug: a missing parameter silently decoupled the sampled gradient from the policy being evaluated, converging confidently to the wrong sign until the exact-gradient check exposed it.

Repository ↗

bandit-exploration-lab

Reinforcement Learning

Multi-armed bandit exploration algorithms (epsilon-greedy, UCB1, Thompson sampling, gradient bandit) compared empirically on stationary and drifting ("restless") bandits rather than by citation alone. Shows constant-step-size estimation more than doubling the optimal-action rate over sample averaging once the environment stops standing still.
